# Accountability content audit

> Status: content approved and implemented 2026-08-27. Accountability is not fully audited until its redesigned magnet is separately approved and live.

This document is the authoritative audit record for the approved Accountability Need content package. It supersedes the imported Accountability copy and Need-level citations in the historical legacy snapshot once the canonical migration in this branch is complete.

## Approved short Evidence copy

> Accountability concerns being able to recognize our part in what happens and respond to the effects of our actions. People judge responsibility by considering how a person was connected to an outcome, what expectations applied, and how much control they had, and we create forms of answerability in which choices or judgments can be explained or justified. How that answerability is structured matters; accountability does not have one uniformly helpful effect. Responsibility itself does not require blame or self-punishment. Clinical work describes treating people as responsible agents while blame is deliberately avoided, and research on interpersonal transgressions finds that taking responsibility can coexist with self-acceptance and efforts to repair. When this Need is active, we may be wanting responsibility to be something that can be acknowledged and responded to rather than ignored or disowned.

## Approved Details

Schlenker and colleagues studied how people judge responsibility. Their model connects a person to an event and to expectations about conduct. In two studies, responsibility judgments changed with the strength of those connections, especially perceived personal control, and people sought information relevant to them when making responsibility judgments. Causal involvement alone was not the whole judgment; the expectations that applied and the person's relationship to the event also mattered.

Lerner and Tetlock reviewed research on accountability arrangements in which people expected to explain or justify judgments and choices. The effects varied with the ground rules and timing. Depending on the conditions, accountability could reduce particular cognitive biases, leave them unchanged, or make them stronger. Their review describes forms of answerability whose effects depend on how they are structured.

Pickard examines clinical treatment in which some behaviors can harm the person or other people. She describes encouraging people to recognize their agency and responsibility while clinicians deliberately avoid hostile blame. In this clinical model, recognizing responsibility and using blame are separate responses.

Woodyatt and Wenzel followed people after interpersonal transgressions and distinguished genuine self-forgiveness from self-punishment and from responses that minimized responsibility. Genuine self-forgiveness involved working through the offense, taking responsibility, and accepting oneself while acknowledging failure. In their prospective research, it was associated with greater self-esteem and empathy toward the other person. Taking responsibility did not require remaining in a self-punitive stance.

## Internal source roles and limits

- **Schlenker et al.:** The triangle model and two studies support the public discussion of how responsibility judgments depend on the actor's connection to an event, relevant prescriptions or expectations, and the actor-event relationship, with control especially important. This work concerns responsibility attribution; it does not establish Accountability as a formally defined basic psychological need.
- **Lerner and Tetlock:** Reviews experimental accountability arrangements in which people expect to justify judgments or actions. Effects vary with the timing and structure of accountability, so this source supports answerability as a studied social arrangement while directly cautioning against treating externally imposed accountability as uniformly beneficial.
- **Pickard:** A clinical-philosophical account of responsibility without hostile blame in treatment. It supports the conceptual separation between treating someone as an agent responsible for behavior and using affective blame. It is not an intervention trial establishing a general outcome of blame-free accountability.
- **Woodyatt and Wenzel:** Prospective studies after interpersonal transgressions distinguish genuine self-forgiveness, self-punishment, and responsibility-minimizing pseudo self-forgiveness. The reported restorative outcomes are associations and should not be presented as proof that taking responsibility causes those outcomes.

No evolutionary source is retained. The audit found adjacent work on norm enforcement, cooperation, punishment, and reputation, but it did not justify a clean claim that a discrete Accountability Need or module evolved for one specific function. The approved package therefore stays with direct responsibility and accountability scholarship.

## Conceptual boundary

Accountability is treated here as a value or motivation around responsibility being recognizable and respondable. The Need does not contain a particular person or mechanism. `They need to take accountability`, `someone needs to hold me accountable`, compelled confession, punishment, monitoring, public exposure, apology, and a particular repair are possible strategies or desired outcomes rather than the Need itself.

This keeps Accountability distinguishable from nearby concepts without claiming formal definitions. Causality concerns what contributed to an outcome. Reliability and Dependability concern consistency or follow-through. Honesty concerns communication aligned with what someone takes to be true. Justice and Fairness concern different questions about treatment, procedures, distributions, wrongs, or redress. These concerns can coexist; the model does not treat the Needs as opponents.

The audit did not approve function lenses. Self-accountability and concern about another person's accountability change the target or context but did not meet the repository's three-part requirement for distinct lived functions, distinct evidence, and distinct practical recognition value.

## Approved static strategy deck

### Map the responsibility

Choose one outcome you want to understand more clearly. List the people and factors that contributed to what happened, including your own actions. Then consider how responsibility is distributed among them. Write the part you believe was yours without taking responsibility for what was outside your control.

Supporting source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7853755/

Internal evidence classification: **clinically grounded translation**. Murray and Ehlers describe cognitive therapy for post-traumatic stress disorder when moral injury is part of the clinical picture. Under `Generating realistic appraisals of responsibility`, they recommend responsibility pie charts that list all people and factors responsible for an outcome, allocate contribution across them, and include the patient last so the patient's role is neither overstated nor understated. The card preserves that responsibility-appraisal action while removing the trauma-treatment context, percentages, and therapist-led procedure. It does not claim that this exact general-purpose Accountability card was trialed.

### Draft a repair

If there is something you believe is yours to address, privately write one repair you could choose to make. It could be an apology or another form of amends. You do not have to send it or contact anyone. The first step is only to identify a repair that fits what you believe you are responsible for.

Supporting source: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/15298861003669565

Internal evidence classification: **direct intervention evidence with a low-risk private translation**. Exline, Root, Yadavalli, Martin, and Fisher evaluated a laboratory-based exercise designed to facilitate reparative behaviors and self-forgiveness. In the randomized second study, a responsibility/repair condition increased the odds that women offered reparative behaviors. The study's repair examples included apology and amends. The card narrows this to privately identifying a possible repair and does not claim that the reader must make contact, apologize, obtain forgiveness, or produce a particular response from another person.

The two-card deck is intentional. `Map the responsibility` supports a more balanced appraisal of one's own responsibility. `Draft a repair` supports considering a response after responsibility has been identified. No additional system card is retained merely to increase deck size.

## Legacy evidence disposition

The imported Accountability Need had no static legacy strategies, so there are no inherited Accountability strategy associations to preserve or remove.

Its Need-level evidence set is rejected and rebuilt rather than migrated:

- `https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7213777/` is Social Safety Theory and is adjacent to trust and social safety rather than direct grounding for the approved Accountability claims.
- `https://bmchealthservres.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12913-023-10035-3` is Weenink and colleagues' qualitative hospital study of accountability mechanisms around work-as-done and work-as-imagined. It is too setting-specific to serve as foundational evidence for the site's broader Need.
- `https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/apps.12008` was incorrectly described in legacy as a meta-analysis connecting accountability and team performance. DOI `10.1111/apps.12008` is Park, Jacob, Wagner, and Baiden's *Job Control and Burnout: A Meta-Analytic Test of the Conservation of Resources Model*, about job control and burnout rather than Accountability.

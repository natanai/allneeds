# Safety Content Audit

> Status: approved and authorized for production implementation on 2026-08-25. This file is the authoritative current Safety content record and supersedes the older Safety section in `docs/content-evidence-review.md`. Safety is not fully audited until an approved production magnet is also live.

## Approved short copy

> Across evolutionary history, avoiding harm depended on detecting danger, taking protective action, and recognizing when conditions were safe enough to return to other activities. We may be drawn to create distance from harm, seek shelter or trustworthy people, set boundaries, reduce exposure, and look for credible signs that danger has passed. Protective responses can change with the situation rather than following one fixed pattern. Tending to Safety can mean responding when danger is present while also noticing when enough safety has returned for rest, exploration, connection, and other goals.

## Approved expanded copy

> Protection from harm is among the oldest problems living organisms have had to solve. LeDoux argues that what humans share with other animals is not necessarily one inherited conscious fear system, but ancient defensive survival functions that detect threats and organize protective behavior. Mobbs and colleagues similarly describe human defense as involving processes such as anticipating danger, preventing exposure, assessing threats, searching for safety, escaping, and responding rapidly when danger becomes immediate. These accounts describe defensive functions rather than one single conscious feeling or one universally agreed psychological need. [1][2]
>
> Protective responding is not one fixed reaction. In a human experiment, Mobbs and colleagues had participants escape from a virtual predator capable of delivering painful stimulation. As the predator became more immediate, patterns of neural activity and participants' experience of the threat changed. The laboratory task does not show that ordinary life follows one exact defensive sequence. It supports the narrower point that protective responding can change with circumstances and with how immediate a threat has become. [3]
>
> Recognizing safety is also important. Laing and colleagues review safety learning as an active process in which the unexpected absence of an anticipated threat can create new associations with safety. Gilbert offers a related distinction among responding to threat, seeking safety, and detecting what he calls safeness: resources and conditions that support coping and allow activities such as exploration to resume. These are theoretical frameworks rather than a universally accepted taxonomy, but together they highlight an important point: protection involves more than detecting danger. It can also involve taking in credible information that harm is not occurring, has ended, or can now be managed. [4][5]
>
> Protective learning is imperfect. Cooper and colleagues' meta-analysis found that conditioned fear generalized more broadly among people with anxiety-related disorders than among comparison participants. Generalization can be useful when similar situations really do share danger, but it can become costly when alarm spreads to harmless cues. This does not mean that someone's sense of danger should be dismissed. Alarm is information worth attending to without having to be treated as proof by itself. [6]
>
> This also keeps Safety distinct from Calm. A person can be frightened or physiologically activated while taking sensible protective action, and feeling calm does not establish that a situation is harmless. Tending to Safety can involve responding to credible danger, reducing exposure to harm where possible, and remaining able to notice when circumstances have changed enough for defensive attention to ease.

## Internal source-role notes

- **[1] LeDoux:** strongest deep-history framing. Ancient defensive survival functions are not equivalent to one inherited conscious fear system and do not establish a discrete psychological need scientifically named Safety.
- **[2] Mobbs et al. 2015:** theoretical synthesis of prediction, prevention, assessment, safety seeking, escape, and rapid defense. Treat the proposed organization as theory, not settled neural fact.
- **[3] Mobbs et al. 2007:** direct human experimental evidence that defensive processing changes with threat imminence. The virtual-predator laboratory task does not establish a universal real-world defense sequence.
- **[4] Laing et al.:** current integrative safety-learning review. Safety learning remains a narrower experimental construct than every everyday experience of safety, and the field lacks one consensus definition.
- **[5] Gilbert:** theory-informed distinction among threat response, safety seeking, and safeness. Useful conceptual framework, not a universally accepted taxonomy.
- **[6] Cooper et al.:** meta-analytic evidence of broader conditioned-fear generalization in anxiety-related disorders. This supports calibrated attention to alarm and safety information, not dismissing an individual's present danger signal as inaccurate.

Christianson et al. (2012) and Fanselow (2022) were retired from the public Safety source set because the approved package uses newer or more directly useful sources for their roles. The van Dis safety-behavior paper was considered but not added because Cooper's meta-analysis provides the needed caution about generalized alarm with a cleaner source-to-claim fit.

- **Title:** Orient with 5-4-3-2-1
- **Needs:** Safety + Clarity
- **Wording:** `If immediate physical action is not needed, look around and name 5 things you can see, 4 things you can feel or touch, 3 things you can hear, 2 things you can smell, and 1 thing you can taste. Go slowly and bring your attention to what is actually around you right now.`
- **Evidence classification:** established clinical practice
- **Supporting source:** https://www.fhft.nhs.uk/patients-and-visitors/patient-information-library/anxiety-information-pack

- **Limitation:** grounding can support present-moment orientation during anxiety/distress; it does not determine whether an environment is objectively safe.

### `slow-breathing-safety`

- **Title:** Slow your breathing
- **Need:** Safety
- **Wording:** `If immediate physical action is not needed and your body is still on high alert, spend about five minutes breathing slowly at a comfortable depth. Keep the breath gentle rather than forcing it. If counting helps, count steadily as you breathe in and out.`
- **Evidence classification:** direct evidence plus established clinical practice
- **Supporting source:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38137060/
- **Public link label:** `Supporting source ↗`
- **Limitation:** supports regulation of stress/anxiety/high arousal. Reduced arousal is not evidence that the environment is safe. The previous unsupported four-seconds-in/six-seconds-out prescription was removed.

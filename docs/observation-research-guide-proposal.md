# Observation research guide — review proposal

Status: **Research and copy proposal, not approved for production**  
Prepared: 2026-10-11  
Scope: The Observation page's *research/evidence disclosure*, not the Quick Check or analysis engine.

## Problem

The guide currently leads with definitions, highlighting explanations, worked examples, and general advice. These duplicate the already-available contextual Quick Check. Its five source links are largely at the bottom, so the reader sees rules rather than what research investigated. The three-line summary treatment (eyebrow/title/description) compounds this visual repetition on all screen widths.

The new public area should foreground **study design, findings, strength and limitations**, not merely collect citations to adjacent topics. It must not present the NVC-informed Observation → Feeling → Need → Strategy sequence as an empirically proven clinical protocol.

## Proposed public heading and entry point

**Research behind observations**

Brief visible orientation:

> Research on behavioral observation, memory, and interpersonal communication helps explain the value and limits of describing events concretely. These studies do not test this app or establish that its observation checklist improves clinical outcomes.

Four compact, single-line disclosure rows follow. The title and one chevron are the only summary contents. No three-line heading stacks, badges masquerading as evidence grades, or multiple nested reference panels.

### 1. What can structured observation establish?

**Method examined.** Nock and Kurtz (2005) reviewed the procedures used for direct behavioral observation in schools: defining a target behavior, describing its context, and coding/recording what happened. This is a *clinical-practice/methods review*, not a randomized treatment-effect study. [1]

**What that supports.** Defining an action and recording its context makes it possible to specify what was observed and how the record was produced. In allneeds, time, setting, actions, and exact words serve as *optional descriptive detail*.

**What it cannot establish.** Neither a carefully written account nor the site's Quick Check independently establishes another person's intentions, the truth of all details, or the clinical benefit of using this specific interface.

### 2. How reliable are behavioral measurements?

**Evidence examined.** Padilla and colleagues (2023) systematically reviewed **46 studies** presenting reliability or validity evidence for **six criterion-referenced behavior-analytic assessments**. The evidence was mixed, and the reviewers found inconsistent measurement vocabulary and methodological limitations. [2]

**Related research practice.** Essig, Rotta, and Poling (2023) reviewed **596 experimental reports** from two behavior-analytic journals (2017–2021). For studies using human observers of participant behavior, **96.4%** reported an interobserver-agreement measure. **54.7%** reported procedural fidelity; among those reporting fidelity, only **17.7%** reported interobserver agreement on that fidelity measure. [3]

**What that supports.** Scientific observation needs a defined recording method and appropriate checks, particularly when observers' records are compared. Agreement is relevant to consistency but is not synonymous with truth, accuracy, or validity.

**What it cannot establish.** These are studies of assessments and research reporting, **not** validation studies for the allneeds keyword detector or proof that a single self-reported event is objectively correct. The percentages refer to publication practices, not participants' clinical improvement.

### 3. What are the limits of remembered events?

**Evidence examined.** Brassil, O'Mahony, and Greene (2024) systematically reviewed **nine studies**, including **23 associations** between adults' measured cognitive abilities and susceptibility to misinformation in eyewitness-memory tasks. Almost all associations linked higher ability with *less* susceptibility, but none established immunity; the authors noted heterogeneous methods and a small eligible evidence base. [4]

**What that supports.** Remembered details and directly recorded details are not necessarily interchangeable. A person can identify a quote as approximate or uncertainty as part of an honest account.

**What it cannot establish.** Eyewitness experiments do not show that this app prevents memory distortion, that more words make an account true, or that someone's experience of harm is invalid.

### 4. Why distinguish an event from an interpretation?

**Evidence examined.** Sanford (2006) observed **77 recently married couples** across **four conflict conversations** and measured their appraisals before each conversation. Within couples, shifts in expectations and attributions predicted changes in communication behavior; expectations of being understood yielded particularly strong associations. [5]

**What that supports.** An appraisal and a description of an event are analytically different, and interpretation is relevant to understanding how people communicate. Writing both can help a reader see what happened **and** what it meant to them.

**What it cannot establish.** This repeated-measures observational study is not a randomized trial of Nonviolent Communication or the allneeds observation step. It does not mean a person's interpretation should be deleted, nor does it identify a universal best way to communicate.

## Visible closing note

**What remains untested here:** None of these five works evaluates the full allneeds/NVC-informed sequence, its Quick Check detector, or whether rewriting an observation in this format improves mental health or resolves conflict. The approach is an *evidence-informed organizing practice*, not an independently validated clinical assessment or treatment. Concrete descriptions should supplement, not disqualify, feelings, interpretation, or reports of harm.

## Evidence audit decisions

| Source | Role and claim fit | Limitations |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nock & Kurtz (2005) | Direct procedural relevance to defining and recording an observation | School/clinical setting; methods paper, not an intervention-effect test |
| Padilla et al. (2023) | Systematic evidence on whether commonly used ABA assessments have adequate reliability/validity support | The six assessments are not the Observation tool |
| Essig et al. (2023) | Quantitative description of observer agreement and procedural-fidelity reporting | Reporting frequency is **not** an estimate of observer accuracy or clinical benefit |
| Brassil et al. (2024) | Systematic evidence that memory is susceptible to post-event misinformation | Eyewitness memory tasks; does not directly evaluate therapeutic journaling |
| Sanford (2006) | Empirical relationship between appraisals and communication during conflict | Married-couple sample and association; not proof that rewriting appraisals causes change |

### Why the existing citations are being reconsidered

- The current IRIS Center guides are useful **instructional resources**, but should not be presented as peer-reviewed experimental evaluations of this process. They can remain in the Quick Check's optional teaching material if they earn a distinct role.
- The current Essig source is relevant to measurement **rigor**, but the previous guide did not tell readers what its 596-paper analysis actually found. The proposal restores its result and the critical caveat.
- Nock & Kurtz remains a directly relevant **clinical methods** source, but not proof of the benefits of NVC.
- The old glossary-like section about ABC, frequency, duration, and latency is informative in specialist settings, yet does not need to dominate this research summary. If it is retained, it should live in optional practical guidance, not be conflated with evidence about the app.
- Do not resurrect previously retired miscited articles merely to increase reference count. A larger library is appropriate only when each additional study addresses a specific claim.

## Proposed interface and verification

- Keep **one shared guide** on mobile and desktop; no device-specific omissions.
- A parent research disclosure has a one-line label; each of the four finding rows has **one title and one chevron**. Expanded details use short bold inline labels for Method, Finding, Interpretation, and Limits rather than subheading stacks.
- Source links can be placed **inside the relevant finding** and repeated in one flat numbered reference library, not a third tier of dropdowns. Reference numbers should remain consecutive and stable until content changes.
- Direct links open the scholarly record or DOI; add accessible names and focus-visible states.
- Long citations should wrap without horizontal overflow at 320px. Headings must stay single-label even when some titles naturally wrap at narrow width.
- Check keyboard/touch disclosure behavior, reference navigation, screen readers, and research/content parity at 390px and desktop.
- Only change production research copy after approval of the complete public wording, five-source set, and described limitations; the present branch changes **row presentation only**, not evidence claims.

## Source-verification state

Reviewed against publisher or indexed records for the five works (bibliographic details, study design, counts and stated limitations). This is a *targeted narrative source audit*, **not** a PRISMA systematic search and **not** clinical validation of allneeds. Before publication, check live URLs and wording once more and record any corrections.

# Human Editorial Style Pass

> Standing editorial rule for final public-facing Need copy, strategy copy, and official full Need audit proposals.

## Purpose

allneeds is transparent that AI may assist with research and drafting. This rule is not about concealing AI use or trying to defeat AI detectors. It exists because readers increasingly associate certain repetitive language patterns with low-trust AI-generated text. In a psychological resource, that reaction can distract from the content and make readers more suspicious of claims that are in fact carefully sourced.

The goal is therefore to remove default model habits while preserving accuracy, accessibility, and the natural voice of the material.

This is an **authoring and editorial review rule**. Do not build a runtime "humanizer," post-processing layer, or second system that repairs canonical copy after it is generated. The final approved text itself must be clean.

## Core rule: content chooses the sentence shape

Do not let a preferred rhetorical pattern determine how many ideas appear in a sentence, how a contrast is framed, or how a paragraph closes. The number of examples, distinctions, clauses, and sentences should come from the content itself.

A final passage should sound like someone carefully explaining this particular subject, not like a reusable template filled with different nouns.

## High-risk AI-style patterns

Treat the following as editing flags. Except where a rule is explicitly absolute, a single occurrence is not automatically wrong. The concern is unnecessary or repeated use.

### Em dashes

Do not use em dashes in proposed or production site copy. This is already a standing allneeds rule and remains absolute.

### Forced groups of three

Do not default to three examples, three adjectives, three consequences, or three parallel nouns because the sentence feels rhetorically complete that way.

Examples such as `preferences, values, or priorities` should contain exactly the distinctions the content needs. If two terms are enough, use two. If four distinct terms are needed, use four. Do not add or remove concepts merely to create a triad.

### Stock contrast formulas

Avoid formulaic constructions such as:

- `not just X, but Y`;
- `not only X, but also Y`;
- `it is not about X, it is about Y`;
- repeated `X is not Y` contrast chains used for rhetorical effect.

A real conceptual distinction may still require a contrast. State it in the most direct form available rather than reaching automatically for a familiar contrast template.

### Generic editorial signposting

Remove phrases that announce importance without adding information, including habitual uses of:

- `it is important to note`;
- `it is worth noting`;
- `this highlights` or `this underscores`;
- `overall`;
- `in summary` or `in conclusion`;
- `taken together` when the paragraph can simply state the synthesis directly.

### Stock elevated vocabulary

Be suspicious of words that make ordinary claims sound more polished or profound without increasing precision. Examples include `delve`, `landscape`, `nuanced`, `robust`, `crucial`, `pivotal`, `meaningful`, `comprehensive`, `seamless`, `transformative`, and similar language.

These words are not banned when they are the exact word the content needs. They should never be used merely to give prose an authoritative tone.

### Repeated scaffolding and polished symmetry

Do not make adjacent paragraphs, lenses, or strategy cards repeatedly follow the same sentence skeleton. Watch for:

- identical opening constructions;
- repeated `This can involve...` lists;
- paragraph after paragraph with the same number and length of sentences;
- symmetrical clauses that exist mainly for rhythm;
- repeated ending sentences that restate the paragraph in slightly grander language.

Let sentence length and paragraph shape vary with the actual evidence being explained.

### Abstract or impersonal phrasing

Prefer direct subjects and verbs when they are available. Do not turn simple ideas into abstract noun-heavy prose merely to sound scholarly.

Public Details can name the researchers, what they studied, and what they found. Strategy cards should tell the reader what they can actually try. Avoid unnecessary distance such as generic references to `individuals`, `one`, or `the user` when ordinary plain language works better.

### Unexplained technical language

Assume no prior education in psychology, therapy, medicine, or research methods.

- Spell out an acronym on first use if the acronym is needed at all.
- Briefly explain a technical term the first time it appears.
- Prefer a plain-language term when the technical label adds no useful precision.

For example, do not write `CBT` and assume the reader knows it means cognitive behavioral therapy. If a technical phrase such as `self-concept clarity`, `processing fluency`, or `role ambiguity` appears, make its meaning understandable from the surrounding sentence.

### Automatic comprehensiveness

Do not pad a description with every nearby concept so that it appears exhaustive. A short Need description should contain the distinctions required by its evidence and purpose, not a catalog of everything that could conceivably relate to the Need.

The same applies to strategies. Prefer one clear action to a card that tries to cover every possible variation.

### Mechanical hedging and walk-backs

Calibrate uncertainty where the evidence requires it, but do not reflexively stack words such as `may`, `can`, `often`, `sometimes`, and `might` in the same passage.

Do not make a broad statement and then retreat from it with a caveat. The Details source-grounding rule still applies: write the narrower accurate statement first.

## Citation prose

Citation-based writing should sound like an explanation of the research, not like an internal evidence review.

Prefer:

- what the researchers examined;
- the population or setting when relevant;
- what they actually found;
- the specific distinction that finding helps the reader understand.

Avoid repetitive phrases such as `this study shows`, `this research highlights`, or `this evidence supports our definition` when the relationship can be made clear through the substance of the paragraph.

Every citation must still meet the separate source-to-claim, citation-foothold, URL, and Details-grounding rules. Removing AI-style language must never weaken evidentiary precision.

## Final read before an official proposal

Before presenting a full, final, official, or approval-ready Need audit proposal, review every piece of proposed site copy for the following:

1. No em dashes.
2. No list or series has been forced into three items for rhythm.
3. Stock contrast formulas and generic signposting have been removed unless the specific content genuinely requires them.
4. Elevated adjectives and abstract language have earned their place.
5. Technical terms and acronyms are understandable to a reader with no prior clinical or academic knowledge.
6. Adjacent paragraphs and cards do not repeat the same rhetorical skeleton.
7. Sentence and paragraph length follow the material rather than a template.
8. No passage repeats an earlier point merely to create a polished closing sentence.
9. Claims remain fully traceable to their citations and no wording has been made stronger for rhetorical effect.
10. The prose reads naturally aloud without adding deliberate mistakes, slang, or artificial quirks to simulate human authorship.

If a phrase is flagged only because it is sometimes associated with AI but it is still the clearest and most precise wording for the content, keep the wording. This is an editorial judgment rule, not a blacklist.

## Why these patterns are reviewed

Contemporary discussion often treats em-dash overuse, stock contrast formulas, three-part lists, generic signposting, and characteristic vocabulary as AI-writing signals. These are not reliable proof that a particular passage was machine-generated. AP Stylebook has specifically cautioned against treating the em dash as a definitive AI tell.

Research is more useful at the pattern level. Russell, Karpinska, and Iyyer (ACL 2025) found that people who frequently used large language models for writing could identify AI-generated nonfiction with high accuracy in their study; their explanations relied on both recurring vocabulary and broader judgments about style. A 2025 survey of linguistic research likewise found recurring reports of more formal or impersonal language, lower lexical diversity, and repetition in AI-generated text, while emphasizing that results vary by model, prompt, language, and genre.

These findings justify a careful human editorial pass. They do not justify treating any one punctuation mark, phrase, or word as proof of AI authorship.
